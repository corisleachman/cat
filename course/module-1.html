Understanding your thoughts

Welcome. This week is about getting to know how CBT works, what your body does when it feels under threat, and starting to notice the thoughts that pop up on their own.

Start here

Before you begin · two minutes

Settle first

Find somewhere you won't be interrupted. Put both feet on the floor and let the chair hold your weight. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of four, and out slowly through your mouth for a count of six. Do this five or six times. Then name five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can feel, two you can smell and one you can taste.

We start every module like this on purpose. A calmer body makes it easier to look at your thoughts with curiosity rather than alarm.

Welcome

Before we start

This is your space. Nobody else will see what you write, and there are no right or wrong answers. Go at whatever pace feels manageable. If a week takes you two weeks, that is fine.

A few things to know:

  • Keep a notebook. A paper notebook or a notes app, just for this course. You will use it every week.
  • Print the worksheets if you can. Writing by hand slows things down in a helpful way. You can also copy them into your notebook.
  • Use the 1 to 10 check-in. Each module starts with it. Over six weeks it shows you how things are shifting.
  • Look after yourself. Some of this may stir things up. Take breaks, and use the support details at the bottom of each page if you need them.

What is CBT

Thoughts, feelings, body, behaviour

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) is built on a simple idea: it is not only what happens to us that affects how we feel, but how we interpret what happens. Our thoughts, feelings, physical sensations and actions are all connected, and they keep each other going.

CBT focuses on the here and now. It gives you practical ways to notice those patterns and gently change them. A useful way to picture it is the “hot cross bun”.

An example

Chris is invited to a party. The situation is simple, but his thoughts race: “People find me boring. If I talk to a stranger I'll blush and make a fool of myself.” He feels anxious. His body feels hot and his heart beats fast. So his behaviour follows: he avoids going if he can, and if he does go, he pretends to text to avoid small talk. Avoiding it brings short-term relief, but it also means he never finds out his fears might not come true, so the cycle continues.

Activity · 15 minutes

Map your own bun

  1. Think of a recent moment that left you feeling anxious, low or stuck. Not the hardest thing, just something recent.
  2. Write the situation in the middle of the blank hot cross bun.
  3. Fill in the four parts: what went through your mind, what you felt, what you noticed in your body, and what you did (or avoided).
  4. Look at how the parts connect. Can you see how each one fed the others?

Your body's alarm

Understanding fight-or-flight

Look at the “physical” part of your bun. A racing heart, shallow breathing, tight shoulders, a churning stomach, feeling hot or shaky. These are not signs that something is wrong with you. They are your body's alarm system, the fight-or-flight response, doing exactly what it was designed to do.

When your brain senses a threat, a small area called the amygdala sounds the alarm. Adrenaline and cortisol flood your system in seconds:

  • Your heart beats faster to pump blood to your muscles.
  • Your breathing speeds up to take in more oxygen.
  • Your muscles tense, ready to act.
  • Digestion slows down, which can cause butterflies, nausea or needing the loo.
  • Your attention narrows onto the threat, and the calmer, thinking part of the brain takes a back seat.

This was brilliant when the threat was a wild animal. Today the alarm goes off for a deadline, a difficult conversation, or simply a frightening thought, and there is nothing to fight or run from. So the energy has nowhere to go, and we feel it as anxiety.

Two things are worth remembering. First, these feelings are uncomfortable but not dangerous, and they always pass. Second, because thoughts can set the alarm off, learning to work with your thoughts can help turn it down. That is what the rest of this course is about.

A first look

Weighing up the evidence

Here is a taste of the main CBT skill you will build. Think of one negative thought from your hot cross bun, the one you believe the most. Now imagine putting it on trial in court.

Draw two columns in your notebook. In one, write the evidence for the thought: facts that support it. In the other, write the evidence against it: facts that don't fit. Only facts count in court, not feelings or guesses.

Don't worry if it feels hard, or if you only find one or two things for each side. You are just practising stepping back and looking. We will go into this properly next week.

This week

Your practice for the week

Homework

Notice · Capture · Write it down

This week, your only job is to become a thought-spotter. Automatic negative thoughts (sometimes called ANTs) are the quick, critical thoughts that pop up on their own: “I'll mess this up”, “they don't like me”, “what's the point”.

  1. Notice when your mood dips or your body tenses. Ask, “what was just going through my mind?”
  2. Capture the thought, word for word if you can.
  3. Write it down with the situation and how you felt. Columns 1 to 3 of the thought record are perfect for this.

You don't need to change anything yet. Just notice.
