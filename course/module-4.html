Module 4 · about an hour

Feelings are not facts

This week is about learning to be objective: noticing when a feeling is being treated as proof, pausing before you react, and lifting low mood through what you do.

Start here

Before you begin · two minutes

Settle first

Find somewhere you won't be interrupted. Put both feet on the floor and let the chair hold your weight. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of four, and out slowly through your mouth for a count of six. Do this five or six times. Then name five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can feel, two you can smell and one you can taste.

We start every module like this on purpose. A calmer body makes it easier to look at your thoughts with curiosity rather than alarm.

Emotional reasoning

“I feel it, so it must be true”

Emotional reasoning is when we treat a feeling as evidence. “I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong.” “I feel anxious, so something bad must be about to happen.” “I feel useless, so I must be useless.”

Feelings are real and they matter. They tell us something important about what we are going through. But they are not facts about the world. Anxiety can be triggered by a thought, by tiredness, by caffeine, by an old memory. Feeling like a failure does not make you one.

Being objective means stepping back and looking at the situation as an outside observer would. Some questions that help:

  • Is this a fact, or my opinion or feeling about it?
  • If a friend told me this, what would I say to them?
  • What would someone who cares about me say about this situation?
  • What's the bigger picture? Will this matter in six months?
  • Is my reaction in proportion to what actually happened?

The STOP skill

Pause before you react

When emotions are running high, it is hard to be objective in the moment. The STOP skill gives you a pause button:

  1. Stop. Don't react. Freeze for a moment, so your emotions don't act for you. Name the emotion.
  2. Take a step back. Take a breath, and keep breathing slowly until you feel more in control. Very few things need a split-second decision.
  3. Observe. What is happening around you and inside you? What automatic thoughts are showing up? What are the facts?
  4. Proceed mindfully. Ask, “what do I want from this situation? What would make it better, or worse?” Then choose your response.

Notice how the “take a step back” part uses the same long, slow breath from Module 2. You are pressing the brake so your thinking brain can catch up.

If your mood is low

Doing more, to feel better

When we feel low, we naturally do less. We withdraw from people, drop hobbies and put things off. It makes sense, because everything feels like effort. But doing less means less achievement, less connection and less pleasure, and that makes us feel lower still.

Behavioural activation breaks the cycle at the point where we do less. Instead of waiting to feel motivated before acting, we act first, in small, planned steps, and the mood often follows.

  1. For a few days, use the activity log to note what you do each hour, and rate each activity for achievement, closeness (connection to others), enjoyment and importance.
  2. Look for patterns. What lifts you, even slightly? What drains you? When are the empty stretches?
  3. Plan in a few more of the activities that lift you, and a few that matter to you, starting small. A ten-minute walk counts.
  4. Do them as planned, whether or not you feel like it, and notice what happens.

This week

Your practice for the week

Homework

How aware are you now?

This week, notice how conscious you have become of your thoughts, feelings and behaviour. Each day, record at least one moment where you noticed what was going on for you: the thought, the feeling, what you noticed in your body, and what you did. Did you catch any emotional reasoning? Did you use STOP?

Keep a mood diary alongside it, rating your mood morning, afternoon and evening, and noting anything that affected it.
