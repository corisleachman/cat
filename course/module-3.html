Module 3 · about an hour

Facing what bothers you

This week you will look at what gets in the way, gently line up the things that bother you, and start to test your worries out in real life.

Start here

Before you begin · two minutes

Settle first

Find somewhere you won't be interrupted. Put both feet on the floor and let the chair hold your weight. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of four, and out slowly through your mouth for a count of six. Do this five or six times. Then name five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can feel, two you can smell and one you can taste.

We start every module like this on purpose. A calmer body makes it easier to look at your thoughts with curiosity rather than alarm.

Recap

How did the challenging go?

Have you been challenging your negative thoughts? Look back at your worksheets and ask yourself:

  • Which thoughts were easiest to challenge, and which were hardest?
  • What got in the way? Forgetting, feeling too upset in the moment, not believing the balanced thought?
  • What is in front of you right now that feels most difficult?

Barriers are normal, and they are useful information. Write them down. Some will be solved by the tools in this module, and some just need more practice. If the alternative thought doesn't feel true yet, that is fine. Belief often follows action, which is what this week is about.

Activity

Your list of bothersome things

Activity · 25 minutes

From least to most

  1. Settle yourself with a few slow breaths. Close your eyes if that feels OK, and picture the things that are bothering you at the moment, big and small.
  2. Write them as a list, in order: the least bothersome at the top, the most bothersome at the bottom.
  3. Go through the list one item at a time, starting at the top. For each one, reflect: what is it about this that bothers me? What am I telling myself about it?
  4. Try to think about it rationally. Use your fact-check questions. Then write down one possible solution or next step, however small.
  5. Stop whenever you need to. You don't have to reach the bottom of the list today.

Starting with the smaller things builds confidence and momentum, so that by the time you reach the bigger ones you have already had some practice.

If anxiety is the main thing for you

You can use the same list as a graded hierarchy. If there is a situation you avoid, break it into steps from least to most frightening. For example, for social anxiety: say hello to a neighbour, make small talk with a shop assistant, go to a short gathering with a friend, go to a party. Then work up the steps one at a time, staying with each until the anxiety starts to settle. Avoidance keeps fear going, while gentle, repeated facing shrinks it.

Behavioural experiments

Testing a worry in real life

Some thoughts are predictions: “if I speak up, I'll go bright red and everyone will notice.” The best way to check a prediction is to test it. A behavioural experiment is a small, planned, safe test of whether a thought is true.

In the worked example, someone believed (95%) they couldn't talk to a stranger without going bright red. They planned to talk to a stranger in a pub, and to be filmed so they could see what really happened. They also wrote down what might stop them, and how they would get past it.

After an experiment, review it. What actually happened? How much do you believe the original thought now? What have you learned?

This week

Your practice for the week

Homework

Do something you love, on purpose

When we are anxious or low, the things that nourish us are often the first to go. This week, choose at least one thing you genuinely enjoy and do it on purpose. Plan it, put it in your diary, and protect the time. Notice how you feel before, during and after.

The butterfly of balance and the mood battery can help you see what drains you and what restores you. Fill one wing (or the top of the battery) with the pressures and stressors in your life, and the other with the things that recharge you. Is there a good balance?

If you feel ready, plan one small behavioural experiment too.
