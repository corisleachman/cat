Module 2 · about an hour

Settling, and fact-checking

This week you will learn how to calm your nervous system on purpose, and then how to tell the difference between a thought that is based on fact and one that is driven by feeling.

Start here

Before you begin · two minutes

Settle first

Find somewhere you won't be interrupted. Put both feet on the floor and let the chair hold your weight. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of four, and out slowly through your mouth for a count of six. Do this five or six times. Then name five things you can see, four you can hear, three you can feel, two you can smell and one you can taste.

We start every module like this on purpose. A calmer body makes it easier to look at your thoughts with curiosity rather than alarm.

Look at the thoughts you captured. Did any patterns come up? Did the same kinds of thoughts appear in the same kinds of situations? Were some easier to notice than others? Write a few lines in your notebook about what you found.

If you found it hard to catch your thoughts, that is completely normal. They are automatic precisely because we are so used to them. Keep going, it gets easier.

The vagus nerve

Your body's built-in brake

Last week we met the fight-or-flight response, your body's accelerator. You also have a brake: the parasympathetic or “rest and digest” system. The vagus nerve is a key part of it. It runs from the brainstem down through your throat, heart, lungs and gut, and it carries messages in both directions. Most of its traffic actually goes from the body to the brain.

That is good news, because it means you can send your brain signals of safety through your body. Some ways that work for many people:

  • A longer out-breath. Breathing out for longer than you breathe in (in for 4, out for 6 or 7) slows the heart. This is the quickest brake you have.
  • Humming or singing. The vibration and slow exhale both help.
  • Cool water. Splash cold water on your face or hold something cold.
  • Soft eyes and a relaxed jaw. Unclench, drop your shoulders, and let your gaze widen.
  • Gentle movement. A slow walk, a stretch, rolling your shoulders.

None of these are magic, and they work best with practice, when you are already fairly calm. Then they are there for you when you need them.

Grounding

Coming back to the here and now

When we are anxious our minds race into the future. When we are low they get pulled into the past. Grounding gently brings your attention back to the present moment through your senses, which helps your nervous system register that, right now, you are safe enough.

The 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 exercise at the start of each module is one form of grounding. Others include feeling your feet pressing into the floor, holding a warm drink and noticing its weight and warmth, or describing an object in front of you in detail.

Our attention is selective. We tend to notice only a small part of what is around us, and when we are stressed it locks onto threats. The worksheet below helps you practise widening it on purpose.

How we see things

Our minds take shortcuts

Our brains are constantly filling in gaps. When we feel anxious or low, they tend to fill them in with the most negative version of events, and to notice the things that confirm how we already feel. Rational or irrational

Fact-check your thoughts

Rational thoughts are based on evidence. They tend to be more balanced, and they often help us feel motivated. Irrational thoughts are based on emotion. They are less reliable, and they tend to create anxiety or low mood.

When you catch a thought that is making you feel worse, fact-check it with three questions:

  1. Is this thought based on fact? Try to “put a camera on it”. If you filmed your day, what would the camera actually show? Sometimes you can't know until the event is over.
  2. Does this thought help me achieve my goals? Or does it get in the way of what I want to do?
  3. Does this thought help me feel how I want to feel? Usually an irrational thought has a negative impact on your feelings, actions and the situation as a whole.

Then ask: can I turn it around? What would a more rational, balanced version of the thought be? Look for what you might have missed, and fact-check it again.

Cognitive restructuring

Challenging negative thoughts

Why challenge a negative thought at all? Because automatic thoughts feel like facts, but they are often guesses, shaped by mood, tiredness, old experiences and that alarm system we met last week. Left unchallenged, they decide how we feel and what we do.

Cognitive restructuring is the process of slowing a thought down and checking it, so you can replace it with something more accurate and balanced. It is not about forcing yourself to “think positive”. It is about being fair.

Look at the worked example first. Someone made a small mistake at work, and their boss pointed it out. Their automatic thought was “my boss is mad and I'm going to get the sack”, which they believed 75%. Once they listed the evidence on each side, a more balanced thought emerged, and their belief in the original thought, and their worry, dropped.

Activity · 20 minutes

Put one thought on trial

  1. Choose one thought from last week's list, ideally the one you believe the most.
  2. Write down the situation, how it made you feel, and how much you believe the thought (0 to 100%).
  3. List the evidence for, and the evidence against. Facts only.
  4. Ask: is there an alternative thought that fits the evidence better? Write it down.
  5. Re-rate how much you believe the original thought now, and how you feel.
